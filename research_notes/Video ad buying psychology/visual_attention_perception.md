Source-quality key used below: **[PRIMARY-ACADEMIC]** peer-reviewed; **[PLATFORM]** published by Meta/Google/TikTok, often with a research partner; **[VENDOR]** commercial measurement or creative firm marketing its own product; **[SECONDARY]** a trade press or blog report of a study whose original I could not fetch. Several platform pages could not be fetched directly: the Meta "Capturing attention in feed" page returned 404 and the Think with Google PDF would not parse. For those, figures come through trade-press coverage and are tagged [SECONDARY].

## 1. Motion and attention: vision science and eye-tracking

### Takeaway
Motion *onset* (something starting to move), looming motion (an object expanding toward the viewer) and translating motion capture attention involuntarily, even when the motion carries no task-relevant information. Constant motion or receding motion is weaker. In ads, an animated face draws more looking than a static one, and where the face looks steers where viewers look.

### Cited Findings
- **Abrams & Christ (2003, *Psychological Science*):** the onset of motion captures attention. Observers showed a processing advantage for objects that had recently *started* to move, even though the motion was uninformative. The authors argue that motion onset, not motion as such, is what captures attention. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [PubMed: Motion onset captures attention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930472/)
- **Franconeri & Simons (2003):** translating and looming stimuli capture attention, and receding stimuli do not. They conclude that abrupt onset is not the only attention-capturing feature, and that motion which implies an immediate behavioural need (an approaching object) is privileged. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [Franconeri & Simons PDF](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/FranconeriSimons_Motion.pdf); summary in [SciELO review: Looming motion and visual attention](https://www.scielo.br/j/pn/a/VDmtz96hS9yVTyrtdh6DFMJ/?lang=en)
- **Debate:** Abrams & Christ later showed that the onset of *receding* motion can also capture attention when it is produced with stereo depth cues. That supports the "onset" account over a "looming only" account. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [WUSTL: The onset of receding motion captures attention (comment on Franconeri & Simons 2003)](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-onset-of-receding-motion-captures-attention-comment-on-franco/)
- **Eye-tracking of banner ads (Palcu et al., 2017, *Frontiers in Psychology*):** with a human face in a banner, participants were more attracted to and looked longer at *animated* banners than static ones. This helps overcome "banner blindness". [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [PMC5454066](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5454066/)
- **Feed-speed recall:** Facebook cited independent Fors Marsh tests showing people can recall mobile News Feed content at a statistically significant rate after **0.25 seconds** of exposure. [PLATFORM via SECONDARY] — [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818)
- **TV-ad eye-tracking (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters):** an eye-tracking study of nearly **2,000 participants across 31 commercials**, analysed moment by moment. Brand presence, brand size, frequency and duration each affected "zapping" (avoidance). [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6322.html); [Wharton PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/reixwira_moment_to_moment_optimal_branding.pdf)

### Inferences
- In practice, the strongest scroll-stopper is a *change*: a cut, an object starting to move, or something expanding toward the camera in frame 1. Continuous ambient motion is weaker, because the visual system responds to the onset rather than to motion in general.
- A push-in or zoom toward the viewer (looming) in the opening frames should capture attention better than a pull-out (receding). The evidence comes from lab tasks, not ad tests, so treat this as a hypothesis to A/B test.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that directly tests motion onset or looming *inside feed video ads* against business outcomes. The link from lab capture to ad performance is inferred.
- I did not retrieve eye-tracking heatmap data specific to mobile feed video (for example Tobii or MediaScience published studies) within the call budget.

## 2. The first 1–3 seconds: scroll-stopping, hooks and early brand impact

### Takeaway
Feed attention is measured in seconds. Facebook's own data put average time per mobile feed item at about 1.7 seconds. A widely cited Facebook/Nielsen analysis found that up to 47% of a video campaign's value is delivered in the first 3 seconds and up to 74% in the first 10. Independent attention vendors report that typical feed ads get under 2 seconds of active attention.

### Cited Findings
- **Facebook/Nielsen:** "up to **47%** of the value in a video campaign was delivered in the first three seconds, and up to **74%** in the first ten". People who watched under 3 seconds still showed lifts in ad recall, brand awareness and purchase intent; one summary cites +47% ad recall, +32% awareness and +44% purchase intent. The study dates from about 2015–2016 and is based on Facebook campaigns. "Value" here means brand-lift metrics, not sales. [PLATFORM via SECONDARY] — [Marketing Dive: Brand lift happens in less than 1 second of video](https://marketingdive.com/news/brand-lift-happens-in-less-than-1-second-of-video-study-finds/377333); [Flowjam summary](https://www.flowjam.com/blog/video-ad-hooks)
- **Facebook:** average time spent with a piece of content is **1.7 seconds on mobile** vs **2.5 seconds on desktop**. [PLATFORM via SECONDARY] — [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818)
- **Amplified Intelligence (Karen Nelson-Field):** one advertiser's (Irish National Lottery) Facebook ads averaged **1.7 active attention seconds**. The firm claims ads need about **2.5 s** of active attention to be remembered and that **85% of online ads** fall short of that. It also reports that ad length does not relate to active attention in scrollable formats. [VENDOR; the 2.5 s threshold and 85% figure are the firm's own, not independently peer-reviewed] — [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/opinion/irish-national-lotterys-attention-research-this-is-why-we-care-so-much-about-distinctive-brand-assets/5792); [Campaign US: Shorter ads deliver better attention on social](https://atwork.marriner.com/wp-content/uploads/Shorter-ads-deliver-better-attention-on-social-media-_-Campaign-US.pdf)
- **Google ABCD (Attention, Branding, Connection, Direction):** Kantar analysis found that ads following the ABCDs saw a **30% lift in short-term sales likelihood** and a **17% lift in long-term brand contribution**. Google says the framework draws on more than 17,000 campaigns. [PLATFORM with Kantar] — [Google ABCD reference guide](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/youtube_google_abcd_reference_guide_en.pdf); [Google Business: ABCDs of effective video ads](https://business.google.com/us/resources/articles/abcds-of-effective-video-ads/)
- **"3 s vs 7 s hook" debate:** practitioner posts disagree about how long the hook window is. This is opinion, not data. [SECONDARY/opinion] — [for.you.com post](https://for.you.com/posts/meta-ad-hook-rules-clash-over-3-seconds-vs-7-seconds-1b7a4958)

### Inferences
- Because a large share of brand lift accrues before a "view" is counted, the brand and core proposition must be legible in seconds 0–3, not saved for an end card.
- Platform-reported "thumb-stop" or hook rates mostly come from advertisers' own dashboards. I found no published platform-wide benchmark for them.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Meta's primary page (the URL now returns 404). The 47%/74% figures are verified only through trade press, and the methodology (sample size, which brand-lift studies) is not visible.
- I found no authoritative public benchmark for "thumb-stop rate" (3-second views ÷ impressions). Figures that circulate (for example "25–30% is good") come from agency blogs and were not verified.
- These Facebook figures are about 10 years old. I found no 2023–2026 Meta update.

## 3. Brand placement timing: early vs late reveal

### Takeaway
On YouTube, putting the brand in the first 5 seconds raises ad recall and awareness but also raises the skip rate: a real trade-off. Two moves reduce the cost. Integrate the logo *on the product* rather than floating it, and "pulse" brief brand appearances through the ad instead of one long early or late block.

### Cited Findings
- **Think with Google (YouTube TrueView analysis):** ads with the brand in the first five seconds (logo or audible mention) show higher ad recall and brand-awareness lift, but people are also more likely to skip them. Google describes this as an inverse relationship between recall and engagement. [PLATFORM] — [Think with Google: Creating YouTube ads that break through in a skippable world](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/creating-youtube-ads-that-break-through-in-a-skippable-world)
- **Logo on product:** viewers are less likely to watch and remember ads with floating logos that are not on the product. Google recommends showing the logo on the product. [PLATFORM] — same source
- **Unskippable Labs, Mountain Dew Kickstart:** three versions were tested. The version with a *lighter brand touch* in the first 5 seconds was skipped less on mobile. [PLATFORM experiment, single brand] — [Unskippable Labs: How much branding on YouTube is too much (PDF)](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/1640/unskippable-labs-how-much-branding-on-youtube-is-too-much.pdf)
- **Google ABCD:** brand presence within the first 5 seconds is associated with **+17% brand lift** (as reported in Google's ABCD material). [PLATFORM via SECONDARY] — [ppc.land summary](https://ppc.land/mastering-youtube-advertising-with-the-abcd-framework/); [Google ABCD guide](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/youtube_google_abcd_reference_guide_en.pdf)
- **Brand pulsing (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters, *Journal of Marketing Research*, 2010):** inserting the brand briefly and intermittently throughout a TV commercial reduced zapping by an average of **8–10%**, compared with long brand exposures at the start or end. Large, prominent, long brand exposures increased avoidance. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6322.html); [Tilburg repository](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/moment-to-moment-optimal-branding-in-tv-commercials-preventing-av/)

### Inferences
- Combined advice for skippable or scrollable formats: show the brand early, but small and integrated (on the product or in the scene), and repeat it briefly. Avoid a big logo slate in seconds 0–2.
- For non-skippable or bumper placements, the skip cost does not apply, so an early prominent brand is likely to be net-positive.

### Gaps
- The Think with Google PDF would not parse, so I could not extract the exact recall and skip percentages or the sample size (number of TrueView ads analysed).
- I did not locate the Nielsen study on brand-timing effects that was requested.

## 4. Faces and people

### Takeaway
Faces, especially animated ones, increase looking time on ads. A face whose gaze points at the product raises looking at the product and, in one experiment, purchase intention. Teixeira et al. (2012, *Marketing Science*) report related findings on emotion-driven engagement, which I did not re-verify here.

### Cited Findings
- **Palcu et al. (2017):** in banner ads, a face whose gaze was directed at the product increased the likelihood of looking at the product, whether the face was animated or static. Gaze toward the product also raised purchase intention for that product. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [PMC5454066](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5454066/)
- **Print-ad literature reviewed in the same paper:** a model gazing toward the product increased dwell time on ads and products and improved memory for product and brand. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC, review] — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00881/full)

### Inferences
- In video, a person's on-screen gaze or gesture toward the product or price in the first seconds is a cheap way to direct attention. A face looking straight at camera captures attention to the face itself, which may compete with the product.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve quantified video-ad data on faces versus no faces (for example Realeyes facial-coding or Google's "close-up faces in the opening" findings). Those numbers were not verified.
- No quantified evidence was gathered on facial emotion (joy/surprise) and retention in digital video, within budget.

## 5. Colour, contrast and typography at mobile sizes

### Takeaway
I found little primary data here. Guidance is mostly practitioner best practice: high contrast, few words per card, about 2 s minimum on screen, designed for vertical. Mayer's coherence and signaling principles (section 7) give the strongest evidence-based rationale for minimal, cue-like on-screen text.

### Cited Findings
- **Practitioner guidance:** 5–8 words maximum per text overlay, shown for at least 2 seconds; high-contrast colours; design natively for 4:5 or 9:16; put key visuals in the first second. [VENDOR/blog, not research] — [Benly: Mobile-first ad design](https://benly.ai/learn/ad-creative/mobile-first-ad-design)
- **Mayer's signaling principle** (cues that highlight the organisation of essential material improve learning): median effect size **d = 0.41** (24 of 28 tests). **Coherence principle** (excluding extraneous material): median **d = 0.86** (23 of 23 tests). [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC, learning context] — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44); [U-Mich multimedia learning PDF](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/um-multimedialearning-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- Short keyword-style supers that signal structure should outperform full-sentence text blocks, which conflict with the coherence principle.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or platform-published data on minimum font size or contrast ratios for mobile video ads, or on colour effects on ad attention. WCAG's 4.5:1 contrast ratio is an accessibility standard, not ad-performance evidence, and was not sourced in this pass.
- The "5–8 words / 2 seconds" rule is uncited practitioner lore.

## 6. Sound-on vs sound-off, captions, music and sonic branding

### Takeaway
"85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" comes from publisher-reported data in a May 2016 Digiday article. It was never a Facebook-wide measurement and is stale. Vendor blogs still repeat it as current, so it should be flagged as a myth. Better-grounded current data: a Verizon Media/Publicis 2019 survey found 69% of US consumers watch with sound off in public and 25% in private. On TikTok, sound is central (88% call it essential; Kantar research commissioned by TikTok). Captions raised view time by about 12% in Facebook's 2016 internal test. Music and sonic cues have strong but mostly vendor-sourced evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **The original "85%" source:** Digiday (May 2016) reported that publishers such as LittleThings and Mic saw about 85% of Facebook video views without sound, and agency MEC North America saw 85–90% for brand clients. This is *publisher/agency-reported*, from the 2016 autoplay-muted era, when Facebook counted a view as 3 seconds. [SECONDARY trade press] — [Digiday: 85 percent of Facebook video is watched without sound](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/)
- **Still repeated as current:** 2025–2026 creative-advice blogs restate "approximately 85 percent of mobile video ads are watched without sound" without new sourcing. [Myth propagation] — [Benly](https://benly.ai/learn/ad-creative/mobile-first-ad-design)
- **Verizon Media + Publicis Media (2019), survey of more than 5,600 US consumers:** **69%** watch video with sound off in public places and **25%** in private places. **80%** are more likely to watch an entire video when captions are available. **80% of caption users are not hearing-impaired.** This is self-reported survey data. [VENDOR/PLATFORM survey] — [Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx); [Beey summary](https://www.beey.io/en/captions-increase-viewership-accessibility-and-reach-23486/)
- **Facebook captions (Feb 2016 internal study):** captions increased video view time by **12%** on average. Facebook also found **80%** react negatively when feed mobile video ads play loudly unexpectedly. A&W Canada reported **+25%** watch time with captions. [PLATFORM via SECONDARY] — [3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/captions-increase-viewership-for-facebook-video-ads/); [exchange4media](https://www.exchange4media.com/digital-news/facebook-introduces-new-updates-for-video-ads-63325.html)
- **TikTok/Kantar:** **88%** of TikTok users say sound is essential to the TikTok experience. **73%** say they would "stop and look" at TikTok ads with audio, higher than on other platforms. TikTok is said to be the only network where ads with sound drive significant lifts in purchase intent and favourability. [PLATFORM-commissioned] — [TikTok for Business: Kantar report](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/tr/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-shares-new-insights-into-the-importance-of-sound-for-marketing-promo/601569)
- **Sonic branding:** a cited Ipsos finding is that sonic brand cues were **8.53×** more powerful than visual brand assets. Other claims: people react **17% faster** to sound than to visuals; brand-fit music gives a **13%** lift in comprehension; recall is **4×** stronger when the music is memorable. These come via vendor and trade sources, and the original Ipsos methodology was not retrieved. [VENDOR via SECONDARY, treat cautiously] — [Advertising Week: Predicting effectiveness, the sonic logo](https://advertisingweek.com/predicting-effectiveness-brand-strategy-and-the-sonic-logo/); [YouGov article](https://yougov.com/articles/40973-invest-sonic-branding-boost-consumer-recall-and-re)
- **IPA / MassiveMusic "Sound Science":** campaigns using music show **20–30%** higher effectiveness, and the right music choice can add another **10–30%**. Based on the IPA effectiveness databank but published by a music agency. [VENDOR with IPA data] — [MassiveMusic IPA SoundScience Report](https://resources.massivemusic.com/hubfs/White%20Papers/MassiveMusic_IPA_SoundScience_Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- The muted share is platform- and context-dependent: high on Meta feeds and in public, low on TikTok and YouTube (sound-on by default). Ads should be built for both modes: the visual track and captions carry the message, and the sound adds lift where it is on.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2022–2026) Meta-published figure for the share of feed or Reels video watched muted. The 85% figure is unverified for today.
- I did not retrieve the primary Ipsos sonic-branding study, so the 8.53× figure is unverified.

## 7. Pacing, cut rate and cognitive load (Mayer and the limited capacity model)

### Takeaway
Faster editing raises arousal and attention and, up to a point, memory. Lang's limited capacity model predicts an inverted U: encoding improves with pacing until arousing content plus pace overload capacity, and then it falls. Mayer's redundancy and coherence principles warn against stacking narration, identical on-screen text and extraneous visuals. That matters for caption-heavy sound-on ads.

### Cited Findings
- **Lang et al., effects of edits:** as the rate of edits (camera changes within the same scene) increases, physiological arousal, self-reported arousal and memory increase. Edits can increase attention to and encoding of content without significantly increasing cognitive load. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC] — [CityU Scholars: The effects of edits on arousal, attention and memory for television messages](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)
- **LC3MP prediction:** arousing content and faster pacing both raise arousal, cognitive effort and encoding "up to the point of cognitive overload", after which effort and encoding decrease. [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC theory] — [BVS/Medline record](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-17763054)
- **Mayer's redundancy principle** (graphics plus narration beats graphics plus narration plus identical on-screen text): median **d = 0.86** across 16/16 tests. Coherence: **d = 0.86** (23/23). Signaling: **d = 0.41** (24/28). [PRIMARY-ACADEMIC, instructional context] — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44); [U-Mich PDF](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/um-multimedialearning-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- Lang's finding that edits within the same scene help attention and memory suggests fast cutting is safest when it does not also introduce new information. Rapid cuts *between unrelated scenes*, combined with dense text and voiceover, risk the overload side of the curve.
- **Tension with sound-off design:** for sound-on viewers, full verbatim captions over narration are "redundant" in Mayer's terms and may hurt comprehension. For sound-off viewers, captions are the only channel. Mayer's work notes that redundancy effects are reduced when text is short. That supports keyword captions or short supers rather than full transcripts when sound is likely on.
- Mayer's effect sizes come from learning experiments with explanatory content and students. Transfer to 6–15 s ads is plausible but untested.

### Gaps
- I found no published platform data (Meta, Google, TikTok) linking cuts per second to view-through or brand recall.
- I did not retrieve specific edit rates (for example cuts per 30 s) at which overload begins in Lang's studies.
