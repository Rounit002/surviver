# Consumer Buying Psychology for Advertising and Short Video Ads

Evidence grades used throughout: **[A]** = large meta-analysis or multi-lab preregistered replication; **[B]** = replicated primary research or large observational databank; **[C]** = single study, contested, or vendor/industry research; **[F]** = failed or shrunk under replication.

## 1. Dual-process models: is most ad effect fast and intuitive?

### Takeaway
The broad idea that a lot of ad processing is fast, low-attention and feeling-led is consistent with the attention data (most ad exposure is passive) and with the IPA emotion findings. But the classic lab evidence for strong *unconscious* behavioural nudges (social/behavioural priming, ego depletion) has largely collapsed under replication. Kahneman himself retracted confidence in the priming chapter. Treat "System 1" as a useful design metaphor, not as proof that subtle cues drive purchases.

### Cited Findings
- [F] Schimmack, Heene & Kesavan computed the R-Index for the 12 articles cited in Chapter 4 (priming) of *Thinking, Fast and Slow*: 11 of 12 scored below 50. Across 31 studies, 100% reported significant results while median observed power was only 57%, an inflation rate of 43%. — [Replication-Index, "Reconstruction of a Train Wreck" (2017)](https://replicationindex.com/2017/02/02/reconstruction-of-a-train-wreck-how-priming-research-went-of-the-rails/)
- [F] Kahneman replied that he "placed too much faith in underpowered studies". He said behavioural priming effects "cannot be as large and as robust as my chapter suggested". — [Replication-Index comment thread](https://replicationindex.com/2017/02/02/reconstruction-of-a-train-wreck-how-priming-research-went-of-the-rails/comment-page-1/); [Mind Hacks summary](https://mindhacks.com/2017/02/16/how-replicable-are-the-social-priming-studies-in-thinking-fast-and-slow/)
- [F] Ego depletion (a staple of the "System 2 is a limited resource" story) failed a 23-lab registered replication (N = 2,141): d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]. — [Hagger et al. 2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science (record)](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/a-multi-lab-pre-registered-replication-of-the-ego-depletion-effec/)
- [B/C] Schimmack also wrote a wider meta-scientific audit of the whole book, which grades chapters by replicability. — [Replication-Index 2020](https://replicationindex.com/2020/12/30/a-meta-scientific-perspective-on-thinking-fast-and-slow/)
- [C, vendor] Amplified Intelligence reports that Facebook ads get about 94% *passive* viewing versus 40% for TV. Active viewing on TV is roughly 2× YouTube and 15× Facebook. Passive (non-attentive) exposure "has an effect" but active viewing is best for sales. — [StopPress interview with Nelson-Field](https://stoppress.co.nz/features/why-pay-ad-s-half-exposed-professor-karen-nelson-field-explains-why-visibility-king/)

### Inferences
- Most short-video exposure is low-attention, so ads have to work through fast recognition (brand assets, faces, emotion, simple claims) rather than argument. That is the defensible core of "System 1 advertising". Its support comes from the attention and effectiveness data, not from the discredited priming literature.
- Avoid design rules that rest on subtle unconscious cues (for example "showing the colour X primes Y behaviour"). That class of effect is the one that replicated worst.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis that directly quantifies "what % of ad effects are System 1". The often-quoted "95% of decisions are subconscious" (Zaltman) has no empirical basis I could locate, so do not use it.
- System1 Group (a vendor) publishes "Star ratings → long-term growth" correlations. I did not fetch primary validation, so treat them as vendor claims.

## 2. Emotion vs reason: the Binet & Field IPA databank

### Takeaway
In the IPA Effectiveness databank (about 1,000 award-entered cases), emotionally led campaigns are associated with roughly twice the profit effects of rational ones, especially over the long term. The often-cited 60:40 brand:activation split is a databank-derived average optimum. It varies by category (B2B is about 46:54). The evidence is large but observational and survivorship-biased, so grade it [B]: robust direction, uncertain magnitude.

### Cited Findings
- [B] *The Long and the Short of It* analysed 996 IPA Effectiveness Award cases from 700 brands across 83 sectors, 1980–2010. — [Alex Murrell summary](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/les-binet-and-peter-field-the-long-and-the-short-of-it); [Rain Agency](https://www.rainagency.com/insights-updates/ten-years-later-the-long-and-short-of-it-research-still-rings-true-today/)
- [B] Emotional campaigns produced more brand effects and more business effects than rational ones. Over the long term they were almost twice as likely to deliver top-box profit growth. — [Alex Murrell summary](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/les-binet-and-peter-field-the-long-and-the-short-of-it)
- [B] Purely emotional campaigns gave a 31% profit uplift versus 16% for purely rational ones; mixed campaigns gave 26%. — [Neuroscience Marketing summary of IPA data](https://www.neurosciencemarketing.com/blog/articles/emotional-ads-work-best.htm); [Alex Murrell, "Thinking rationally about emotion"](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/articles/thinking-rationally-about-emotion)
- [B] The optimum average split is about 60% brand building to 40% sales activation. — [Alex Murrell summary](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/les-binet-and-peter-field-the-long-and-the-short-of-it)
- [B] Later work, *Effectiveness in Context* (IPA, 2018), shows the optimal split varies by context. In B2B the optimum is about 46% brand to 54% activation. — [IPA](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/effectiveness-in-context); [System1 blog summary](https://system1group.com/blog/saved-the-baby-binet-and-field-on-effectiveness-in-context); [CustomerThink](https://customerthink.com/unconventional-views-on-b2b-growth/)
- [B] *Media in Focus* (2017) updated the analysis for the digital era. — [WARC event report](https://www.warc.com/content/article/event-reports/new-lessons-from-binet-and-field---media-in-focus-marketing-effectiveness-in-the-digital-era/en-gb/113293); [System1 blog](https://system1group.com/?p=2277)

### Inferences
- Caveats on the evidence:
  - The cases are self-selected award entries, so successes are over-represented.
  - Effects are self-reported by the entrant agencies.
  - The design is correlational, not experimental, so emotion may be confounded with budget, share of voice and campaign duration.
- A short-video rule this supports: lead with an emotional or feeling payoff and keep rational claims secondary. Pure activation creative ("buy now, X% off") is for short-term response and should not be the whole content mix.
- For a $29 low-involvement, impulse-style product, the activation share can reasonably run higher than 40%, by analogy with the context-dependence findings. This is an inference, not a measured number.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the exact *Effectiveness in Context* ratios for other categories (for example, new versus established brands, or financial services).
- No independent experimental replication of the 31% vs 16% figure exists. It is databank-internal.

## 3. Cognitive biases used in marketing: key studies, effect sizes, replication status

### Takeaway
The robust biases are anchoring, loss aversion (but about λ ≈ 2, not the folklore 2.5+ and not universal), scarcity (meta-analytically positive, but moderated), the left-digit effect, and peak-end (see section 5). Fragile or context-bound: decoy/attraction (largely vanishes with realistic pictorial stimuli) and descriptive-norm social proof (the hotel-towel advantage did not replicate cleanly). Several Cialdini-style principles (reciprocity, authority, commitment) were not verified with modern effect sizes in this session.

### Cited Findings

**Anchoring [A]**
- Anchoring replicated strongly in Many Labs 1 (about 5,000+ participants, four estimation problems) and was among the largest effects in the project, larger than the original studies. — [LessWrong summary of Many Labs](https://lesswrong.com/posts/eXwpbnSeRHTahvJAx); [arXiv overview of large-scale replication projects](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.06031)
- Caveat: anchoring on *preferential* judgments (for example, willingness to pay) has given less consistent results than anchoring on numeric estimates. — [Judgment and Decision Making, 2019](https://doaj.org/article/936066f6a81d4e44987fea134d3451d4); [FORRT replication atlas, incidental anchors](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1002/bdm.586/) (incidental and environmental anchors are weaker)

**Loss aversion [A, magnitude shrunk]**
- Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2024, *Journal of Economic Literature*), meta-analysis of 607 estimates from 150 articles:
  - Mean λ = 1.955, 95% interval [1.820, 2.102].
  - Unadjusted median 1.69.
  - This is lower than Tversky & Kahneman's (1992) 2.25.
  - Earlier meta-analyses found λ = 1.49–1.73 (Neumann & Böckenholt 2014) and 1.31 (Walasek, Mullett & Stewart 2018).
  — [AEA / JEL](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221698); [Healthcare Economist summary](https://healthcare-economist.com/2024/06/26/empirical-estimates-of-loss-aversion)

**Scarcity / urgency [A, moderated]**
- Meta-analysis (*Journal of Retailing*, 2022) of 416 effect sizes from 131 studies:
  - Scarcity cues generally increase perceived value and purchase intention.
  - Demand-based scarcity works best for utilitarian products, supply-based for experiences, and time-based for high-involvement products.
  — [Monash research portal](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/scarcity-tactics-in-marketing-a-meta-analysis-of-product-scarcity/); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.Org/a/eee/jouret/v98y2022i4p741-758.html)
- A second meta-analysis (Ladeira et al. 2023, *Psychology & Marketing*):
  - Purchase likelihood rises under *demand* scarcity ("selling fast") more than supply scarcity.
  - Pure urgency scarcity (limited quantity or limited time) did **not** significantly increase purchase likelihood.
  - Quantity limits create more perceived scarcity than time limits.
  — [Oxford Brookes repository PDF](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/609e47cf-0367-4af7-82ac-9ab7154e2a2c/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Ladeira.pdf)

**FOMO**
- No meta-analytic effect size found. FOMO in marketing is mostly operationalised as scarcity plus social-proof cues (see Gaps).

**Social proof / descriptive norms [B, mixed replication]**
- Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008): "75% of guests reused towels" beat the standard environmental message, and "guests in this room" was the strongest version. — [AcaWiki summary](https://acawiki.org/A_Room_with_a_Viewpoint:_Using_Social_Norms_to_Motivate_Environmental_Conservation_in_Hotels)
- Bohner & Schlüter (2014, *PLoS ONE*) replication in two German hotels:
  - Both message types raised reuse versus no message.
  - The descriptive-norm message was **not** more effective than the standard message.
  - Proximity effects were inconsistent.
  — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118982)

**Decoy / attraction effect [F / contested]**
- Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, *Journal of Marketing Research*) ran many well-powered replications:
  - The effect appeared when attributes were presented numerically, but largely not with pictorial or realistic stimuli.
  - They questioned the effect's practical validity.
  - Huber, Payne & Puto (2014) and Simonson (2014) replied that it holds when its preconditions are met (a clear dominance relation that is easy to perceive).
  — [Huber, Payne & Puto JMR 2014 PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf); [Replication-crisis summary (secondary)](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/decoy-effect-asymmetric-dominance/)

**Left-digit / charm pricing [B]**
- Thomas & Morwitz (2005, *Journal of Consumer Research*, five experiments):
  - A 9-ending price is perceived as meaningfully cheaper than a price one cent higher only when the leftmost digit changes ($2.99 vs $3.00, not $2.89 vs $2.90).
  - The effect is stronger when compared prices are close.
  - It generalises to non-price numbers.
  — [IDEAS / JCR record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2005/06/050607030351.htm)
- Follow-up work replicates the "left digit must change" condition and extends it to 7-endings. — [NTU Management Review](https://review.management.ntu.edu.tw/ebook/26.2/HTML/assets/basic-html/page-93.html)

### Inferences
- **Scarcity:** prefer *demand* signals ("X people bought this week", "selling fast") over countdown timers. The meta-analytic evidence for pure time pressure is weak. This also fits a click-ranked lineup product, where rank and click counts are natural demand cues.
- **Pricing:** $29 already uses a left-digit drop relative to $30. Moving to $29.99 or $28 adds nothing predictable from this literature.
- **Loss framing:** expect losses to weigh about 2× gains, not more. Loss framing ("don't miss your spot") is supported but modest.
- **Decoys:** decoy price tiers only plausibly work when shown as explicit numeric comparisons (a pricing table), not as visual product shots.

### Gaps
- **Not verified this session** (do not cite numbers without checking primaries):
  - Reciprocity (Regan 1971; restaurant mint study, Strohmetz et al. 2002).
  - Authority (Milgram-derived; expert endorser meta-analyses).
  - Commitment/consistency (foot-in-the-door meta-analyses, which report small effects of about r ≈ .1–.2).
  - Charm-pricing field effects (Anderson & Simester 2003 mail-order tests).
  - FOMO scales.
- Many Labs did **not** test scarcity, decoy or reciprocity. Of the classic marketing biases, only anchoring (ML1) and framing were in the first round.

## 4. Trust formation, credibility cues, processing fluency and familiarity

### Takeaway
Ease of processing (fluency) raises liking, perceived beauty and perceived truth. Repeating a claim measurably increases belief in it (the illusory-truth effect, meta-analysed across 51 studies). This is the best-supported mechanism behind "familiar = trusted". Credibility-cue effect sizes specific to video ads were not found in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- [A/B] Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*):
  - Aesthetic pleasure increases with processing fluency.
  - Figural goodness, contrast, symmetry, repetition and prototypicality all act through fluency.
  - Easier-to-read statements are judged more true.
  — [PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)
- [A] Dechêne et al. (2010) meta-analysed 51 studies of the repetition-induced truth effect. Repeated statements are rated more true both within items (second exposure versus first) and between items (repeated versus new). — [edoc Basel record](https://edoc.unibas.ch/10139)
- [A] Fluency and familiarity are also the mechanism behind mere exposure (section 5).

### Inferences
- **Design rules:**
  - High contrast and large legible captions.
  - One message per shot.
  - Consistent brand assets and repeated core claims across ads.
- Each of these raises fluency, and with it liking and believability.
- Repetition raises perceived truth even for claims people have reason to doubt, which is an ethical constraint as well as a tactic.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the exact effect sizes from Dechêne et al. They are believed to be in the d ≈ 0.4–0.5 range, but this was not verified, so check the paper before citing.
- No meta-analytic numbers were gathered on testimonials, UGC-style creators versus brand voice, face-to-camera trust, or ratings and review counts in video. This remains a gap.

## 5. Memory encoding: mere exposure, distinctive assets, mental availability, peak-end, isolation, wear-out

### Takeaway
Repeated exposure reliably increases liking (Bornstein r = .26 across 208 experiments), with an inverted-U that peaks and then declines (wear-out). The peak-end rule is one of the strongest effects in this whole area (r = .58). Ehrenberg-Bass frames brand growth around mental availability: being thought of in buying situations via category entry points, and being recognised instantly via distinctive assets that score on both fame and uniqueness.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Bornstein (1989, *Psychological Bulletin*): meta-analysis of 208 experiments, exposure–liking r = 0.26. Satiation produces an inverted-U. — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html); [Murdoch re-examination record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-re-examination-of-the-mere-exposure/991005635870307891)
- [A] Montoya et al. (2017, *Psychological Bulletin*): 268 curve estimates from 81 articles. They confirm a positive linear term with a negative quadratic term, an inverted-U in which liking rises and then falls with more exposures. — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10198302)
- [A] Alaybek et al. (2022, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*), peak-end meta-analysis:
  - 174 effect sizes; peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations r = 0.581 (95% CI .487–.661).
  - Comparable to the mean of the whole experience.
  - Stronger than first moment, trend or duration (duration neglect).
  — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)
- [B, Ehrenberg-Bass] Distinctive Asset Grid (Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*):
  - An element is a usable asset only if it has *fame* (most category buyers link it to the brand) and *uniqueness* (it evokes only that brand).
  - Marketers' own judgements of asset strength are rarely accurate.
  — [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketers-judgement-strength-brand-assets-rarely-accurate/); [Ehrenberg-Bass/MarketingScience](https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction)
- [B] Mental availability is built through category entry points: link the brand to the situations and needs in which people buy, rather than pushing differentiation claims. — [Marketing Architects summary](https://www.marketingarchitects.com/blog/v41-n3-your-brand-assets-may-be-working-against-you)
- [C, vendor] Nelson-Field / VCCP / Amplified: well-branded ads drive about a third more memory and sell nearly 2× as much per second. — [LBB Online](https://lbbonline.com/news/good-branding-beats-good-creative-dooh-qms-dr-karen-nelson-field)

### Inferences
- **Video rules:**
  - Put the brand asset early and keep it consistent (colour, sound, mascot, typeface).
  - Engineer one clear emotional or "wow" *peak* and a strong *end*. These dominate memory more than length or average quality, so cutting length costs little.
  - Rotate executions to stay on the rising part of the mere-exposure curve.
- **von Restorff (isolation effect):** one visually or audibly isolated element per ad (for example, the price or the brand) should be better remembered. This is a classic list-memory effect and was not verified for ads here.

### Gaps
- I found no verified numbers this session for:
  - Wear-out thresholds (how many exposures before decline) for video ads.
  - The von Restorff effect in advertising contexts.
  - Ehrenberg-Bass quantitative results (for example, the Double Jeopardy law).

## 6. Attention economics

### Takeaway
Most ad exposure, especially on social feeds, gets little or no active attention. Amplified Intelligence (a vendor whose founder sells the attention metric) reports that brand memory can be encoded with about 1.5 seconds of active attention if brand assets are highly fluent. That is down from an earlier 2.5-second threshold. The direction is plausible and consistent with the Ehrenberg-Bass memory findings, but the specific thresholds are [C]: commercially produced and not peer-replicated.

### Cited Findings
- [C, vendor] TV averages about 58% attention per second. Active viewing on TV is about 2× YouTube and 15× Facebook. Facebook is 94% passive versus 40% for TV. Ads below 100% pixels on screen lose attention and sales. — [StopPress](https://stoppress.co.nz/features/why-pay-ad-s-half-exposed-professor-karen-nelson-field-explains-why-visibility-king/)
- [C, vendor] VCCP Media × Amplified: 1.5 seconds of active attention can encode a brand memory when "asset fluency" is high. The previously accepted threshold was 2.5 seconds. — [Creative Salon](https://creative.salon/articles/features/vccp-media-dr-karen-nelson-field-hacking-the-attention-economy); [Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/attention-hacking-creative-media/)
- [C] Attention is a three-part measure: active viewing, passive viewing and avoidance. — [AdNews](https://adnews.com.au/opinion/pay-attention-to-attention-and-your-brand-will-grow)

### Inferences
- **Short-form design rule:**
  - Brand plus core message must land inside the first ~1.5–2.5 seconds.
  - Build for sound-off and partial-screen viewing.
  - Do not save the brand reveal for the end, because most viewers will have scrolled away.
  - Use distinctive assets so recognition does not depend on reading a logo.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed independent replication of the 1.5 s or 2.5 s thresholds was found.
- Amplified's methods (device-camera eye and face tracking on opt-in panels) were not independently audited in sources I retrieved.
- Platform-level numbers are several years old (circa 2019–2021) and may not reflect TikTok/Reels-era behaviour.
