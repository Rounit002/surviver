# Win the first two seconds, then prove it

People mostly decide to buy through fast recognition and feeling rather than through argument. Ads persuade by getting noticed for a second or two, linking that moment to a brand the viewer can recognise without effort, and leaving one strong peak and a clean ending in memory. Video and motion graphics help when the motion carries the message. They hurt when the motion is decoration, too fast, or stacked with redundant text. The most solid evidence is meta-analytic: repeated exposure raises liking (r = .26, with eventual wear-out), memories are dominated by the peak and the end (r = .58), repeated claims are believed more, buyers weigh losses about 2× gains, and animation improves comprehension by a modest d ≈ 0.2–0.4. Almost everything else is weaker than its reputation. Most platform and neuromarketing evidence (Google ABCD, System1, Nielsen, Ipsos, Meta, TikTok, Kantar) comes from firms selling media or ad tests, and it often validates their own predictive models rather than audited sales. Several famous findings are stale myths or failed replications: behavioural priming, ego depletion, "85% watch without sound", "charts make claims believable", and "animation boosts recall 40%". For Surviver.lol the implication is concrete. Founders buying a $29 spot are sceptical of vanity metrics and bot traffic, and they want a relevant audience and verifiable clicks. The winning motion ad therefore opens on a real, moving leaderboard with real numbers, shows the brand integrated rather than slated, pairs a "32 spots" quantity cue with proof from the live stream, and ends on a single CTA. Its claims must be auditable, because this audience punishes anything that looks inflated. Surviver's biggest asset is its distribution: the owner reports **about 400,000 monthly views across daily live streams**, from developers, entrepreneurs and students who use the latest AI tools. Those viewers are the likely users of the products shown. That answers the objection founders raise most often, "wrong audience", so the ads should lead with audience fit and daily exposure, and should back the 400K figure with a verifiable source.

**Evidence grades used in this report:** **[A]** meta-analysis or multi-lab preregistered replication. **[B]** replicated primary research, peer-reviewed field or eye-tracking work, or large observational databanks. **[C]** vendor, platform-commissioned, single-study, aggregator or anecdotal evidence. **[F]** failed or shrunk under replication. **[Inference]** a design rule reasoned from evidence but not tested in ads.

## Buyers decide fast, but not "unconsciously"

The defensible core of "System 1 advertising" is that most ad exposure gets little active attention, so ads have to work through fast recognition. The lab evidence often used to justify it has largely collapsed. An R-Index audit of the 12 articles cited in the priming chapter of *Thinking, Fast and Slow* found **11 of 12 scored below 50**, with 100% significant results despite a median observed power of only 57%. Kahneman conceded that he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies ([Replication-Index](https://replicationindex.com/2017/02/02/reconstruction-of-a-train-wreck-how-priming-research-went-of-the-rails/); [Mind Hacks](https://mindhacks.com/2017/02/16/how-replicable-are-the-social-priming-studies-in-thinking-fast-and-slow/)) **[F]**. Ego depletion, the "System 2 is a limited resource" pillar, failed a 23-lab registered replication with **d = 0.04** ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/a-multi-lab-pre-registered-replication-of-the-ego-depletion-effec/)) **[F]**. The popular claim that "95% of decisions are subconscious" has no locatable empirical basis, so it is excluded here. The useful version of dual-process thinking rests on attention data. Amplified Intelligence reports that Facebook ad viewing is about **94% passive versus 40% for TV**, and that active viewing on TV is about 2× YouTube and 15× Facebook ([StopPress](https://stoppress.co.nz/features/why-pay-ad-s-half-exposed-professor-karen-nelson-field-explains-why-visibility-king/)). Treat this as **[C]**, because the measure is sold by its founder. Treat "System 1" as a design metaphor (be instantly recognisable) and not as licence to rely on subtle unconscious cues such as "this colour primes that behaviour". That class of effect replicated worst.

### Emotion leads in the databank, though the evidence is observational

Binet and Field's analysis of **996 IPA Effectiveness Award cases** from 700 brands found that emotional campaigns produced more brand and business effects, and were almost twice as likely to deliver top-box long-term profit growth ([Alex Murrell summary](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/les-binet-and-peter-field-the-long-and-the-short-of-it)). Purely emotional campaigns showed a **31% profit uplift versus 16% for purely rational ones** and 26% for mixed ([Neuroscience Marketing](https://www.neurosciencemarketing.com/blog/articles/emotional-ads-work-best.htm)). The famous 60:40 brand-to-activation split is an average, and it moves with context: in B2B the optimum is about **46:54** ([IPA](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/effectiveness-in-context); [System1 summary](https://system1group.com/blog/saved-the-baby-binet-and-field-on-effectiveness-in-context)). Grade this **[B]**: the direction is robust and the magnitude uncertain. The cases are self-selected award entries with agency-reported results, and emotion may be confounded with budget and campaign duration. No experimental replication of the 31% vs 16% figure exists. For a $29 impulse purchase, a higher activation share than 40% is reasonable by analogy with the B2B finding, but that is an inference. The B2B evidence points the same way. CEB/Google's study of about 3,000 B2B buyers found that personal value (professional, social and emotional benefits, plus avoiding wasted time and lost credibility) had **twice the impact of business value**. Buyers who saw personal value were **3× more likely to purchase** (71% vs 22.6%) ([CEB/Google whitepaper](https://betaisthenewnormal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/CEB_Google_promotion-emotion-whitepaper-full_beta_2018.pdf)) **[B, but circa 2013 and covering large considered purchases]**.

### Which biases survive replication

Bias evidence splits cleanly into robust and fragile. **Anchoring** replicated strongly in Many Labs 1 and was among the project's largest effects ([arXiv overview](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.06031)) **[A]**, but anchoring on willingness to pay is less consistent than anchoring on numeric estimates ([JDM 2019](https://doaj.org/article/936066f6a81d4e44987fea134d3451d4)). **Loss aversion** holds, but it is smaller than the folklore suggests. A 2024 *Journal of Economic Literature* meta-analysis of 607 estimates puts the mean λ at **1.955**, below Tversky and Kahneman's 2.25 ([AEA/JEL](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221698)) **[A, magnitude shrunk]**. **Scarcity** works on average across 416 effect sizes ([Journal of Retailing meta-analysis](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/scarcity-tactics-in-marketing-a-meta-analysis-of-product-scarcity/)). A second meta-analysis, however, found that **demand scarcity ("selling fast") raises purchase likelihood while pure time-limited urgency does not significantly do so**, and that quantity limits create more perceived scarcity than time limits ([Ladeira et al. 2023](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/609e47cf-0367-4af7-82ac-9ab7154e2a2c/1/Psychology%20and%20Marketing%20-%202023%20-%20Ladeira.pdf)) **[A, moderated]**. The **left-digit effect** is real only when the leftmost digit changes, as in $2.99 versus $3.00 ([Thomas & Morwitz 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html)) **[B]**. On the fragile side, **descriptive-norm social proof** did not replicate cleanly. In a German hotel replication the "most guests reuse towels" message was no better than a standard message ([Bohner & Schlüter 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118982)) **[B, mixed]**. The **decoy effect** largely vanished with pictorial or realistic stimuli in well-powered replications. Its defenders argue that it holds only when the dominance relation is clear and numeric ([Huber, Payne & Puto 2014](https://people.duke.edu/~jch8/bio/Papers/HuberPaynePutoJMR%202014.pdf)) **[F/contested]**. Reciprocity, authority and commitment effect sizes were not verified for this report and are left out.

### Trust comes from ease and repetition; memory comes from peaks and assets

The best-supported route from "familiar" to "trusted" is processing fluency. Contrast, symmetry, repetition and prototypicality make stimuli easier to process, which raises liking. Easier-to-read statements are also judged more true ([Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)) **[A/B]**. A meta-analysis of 51 studies confirms that **repeated statements are rated more true** ([Dechêne et al. 2010](https://edoc.unibas.ch/10139)) **[A]**. That is an ethical constraint as much as a tactic: repetition inflates belief in doubtful claims too. **Mere exposure** raises liking at **r = .26 across 208 experiments** ([Bornstein via Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/mere-exposure-effect.html)). Montoya et al. confirm an inverted U, in which liking rises and then falls with more exposures ([Montoya et al. 2017](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10198302)) **[A]**. Creative therefore needs rotation, although no verified wear-out threshold for video exists. The single strongest memory effect in the notes is **peak-end**. Across 174 effect sizes, the peak and end moments predict retrospective evaluation at **r = .58**, more strongly than the first moment, the trend or the duration ([Alaybek et al. 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)) **[A]**. Ehrenberg-Bass adds that a brand element works as an asset only if it has both *fame* and *uniqueness*, and that marketers misjudge their own assets' strength ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketers-judgement-strength-brand-assets-rarely-accurate/)) **[B]**. Vendor attention research claims that **about 1.5 seconds of active attention can encode a brand memory** when assets are highly fluent, down from an earlier 2.5-second threshold ([Creative Salon](https://creative.salon/articles/features/vccp-media-dr-karen-nelson-field-hacking-the-attention-economy)). The direction fits the memory literature, but the thresholds have not been replicated by peers **[C]**.

## Most viewers give an ad under two seconds, so the opening carries the persuasion

Vision science explains what stops a thumb. **Motion onset** (something starting to move) captures attention involuntarily, even when the motion is uninformative ([Abrams & Christ 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930472/)). **Looming and translating motion** capture attention, while receding motion generally does not ([Franconeri & Simons 2003](https://visualcognition.psych.northwestern.edu/publications/FranconeriSimons_Motion.pdf)). There is a debate over whether onset alone explains this ([WUSTL comment](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-onset-of-receding-motion-captures-attention-comment-on-franco/)) **[B, lab tasks]**. The practical reading is that a *change* in frame one (a cut, an element starting to move, a push-in toward the viewer) beats continuous ambient motion. Nobody has yet tested this against ad outcomes, so it is an A/B hypothesis and not a proven rule.

The window is brutally short. Facebook reported average time per mobile feed item of **1.7 seconds** ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818)). A Facebook/Nielsen analysis found that **up to 47% of a video campaign's brand-lift value arrived in the first 3 seconds and up to 74% in the first 10** ([Marketing Dive](https://marketingdive.com/news/brand-lift-happens-in-less-than-1-second-of-video-study-finds/377333)). Grade this **[C]**: it is platform data reported second-hand, about a decade old, with an invisible methodology, and it measures brand lift rather than sales. Amplified claims that **85% of online ads fall short of the 2.5 seconds of active attention needed to be remembered** ([WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/opinion/irish-national-lotterys-attention-research-this-is-why-we-care-so-much-about-distinctive-brand-assets/5792)) **[C, vendor threshold]**. These sources disagree on exact numbers but agree on the direction: brand and proposition must be legible in seconds 0–3, not saved for an end card.

### Brand early, but small, integrated and pulsed

Brand timing involves a real trade-off. Google's TrueView analysis found that showing the brand in the first five seconds raises ad recall and awareness but also raises skipping. Floating logos that are not on the product reduce both watching and remembering ([Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/creating-youtube-ads-that-break-through-in-a-skippable-world)) **[C, platform]**. In one Unskippable Labs test, a lighter brand touch in the first five seconds was skipped less on mobile ([Unskippable Labs](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/1640/unskippable-labs-how-much-branding-on-youtube-is-too-much.pdf)) **[C, single brand]**. The strongest independent evidence comes from eye-tracking about 2,000 viewers across 31 commercials. **Pulsing the brand briefly and repeatedly cut zapping by 8–10%** compared with long brand blocks at the start or end, and large, long, prominent brand exposures increased avoidance ([Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters via HBS](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6322.html)) **[B]**. Nielsen's neuroscience work adds a cautionary case: during a final branding moment, viewers' eyes stayed on the characters and missed the brand ([Nielsen case study](https://develop.nielsen.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2019/04/case-study-optimizing-creative-using-consumer-neuroscience.pdf)) **[C]**. The synthesis is to put the brand *where the viewer is already looking*, early and at modest size, and repeat it briefly.

### Faces steer gaze; type should signal, not explain

Faces, especially animated ones, increase looking time in ads. In banner eye-tracking, **a face gazing at the product raised looking at the product and raised purchase intention** ([Palcu et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5454066/)) **[B, banners not video]**. A face staring straight at the camera captures attention for itself. Orlando Wood's coding classes "the stare" and abstracted close-ups as features associated with weaker ads ([LBB Online](https://lbbonline.com/news/are-ads-becoming-too-far-left-brain)) **[C]**. Primary data on colour, contrast and font size for mobile video ads is essentially absent. The popular rule of "5–8 words per overlay, held for 2 seconds" is uncited practitioner lore ([Benly](https://benly.ai/learn/ad-creative/mobile-first-ad-design)). The best evidence-based rationale for minimal text comes from multimedia learning research. Removing extraneous material (the **coherence principle, median d = 0.86**) and signalling structure (the **signaling principle, d = 0.41**) both improve comprehension ([Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)) **[A in learning contexts; transfer to 15-second ads is untested]**. Subtitle standards give the best proxy for reading speed: **Netflix allows up to 20 characters per second and 42 characters per line; the BBC uses about 160–180 words per minute** ([Titlevision](https://titlevision.dk/sm/reading_speed.htm); [Nimdzi](https://www.nimdzi.com/speed-in-audiovisual-translation/)) **[guideline]**. Kinetic type adds motion load, so key claims should run slower than those limits.

### "85% watch muted" is a 2016 myth; design for both sound modes

The ubiquitous "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" comes from **publisher- and agency-reported figures in a May 2016 Digiday article**, from the muted-autoplay era. It was never a Facebook-wide measurement ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/)), yet 2025–2026 blogs still repeat it as current ([Benly](https://benly.ai/learn/ad-creative/mobile-first-ad-design)) **[myth]**. Better-grounded data: in a 2019 survey of 5,600+ US consumers, **69% watch with sound off in public and 25% in private**, and 80% of caption users are not hearing-impaired ([Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx)) **[C, self-report]**. Facebook's 2016 internal test found that captions raised view time by **12%** ([3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/captions-increase-viewership-for-facebook-video-ads/)) **[C]**. Sound behaves very differently by platform. **88% of TikTok users call sound essential** ([TikTok/Kantar](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/tr/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok)) **[C, platform-commissioned]**, and Google describes YouTube as almost entirely sound-on ([Think with Google](https://business.google.com/in/think/future-of-marketing/youtube-video-ad-creative/)). Sonic-branding claims such as "sonic cues are 8.53× more powerful than visual assets" trace to vendor and trade sources with no retrievable Ipsos methodology ([Advertising Week](https://advertisingweek.com/predicting-effectiveness-brand-strategy-and-the-sonic-logo/)) **[C, unverified]**. The IPA/MassiveMusic analysis linking music to **20–30% higher effectiveness** draws on databank data but was published by a music agency ([MassiveMusic](https://resources.massivemusic.com/hubfs/White%20Papers/MassiveMusic_IPA_SoundScience_Report.pdf)) **[C]**. The rule that survives is to let the visuals and captions carry the message and let sound add lift where it is on.

### Faster cutting helps until it overloads

Increasing the rate of edits within a scene raises arousal, attention and memory without significantly increasing cognitive load ([Lang et al.](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)) **[B]**. Lang's limited-capacity model predicts that encoding falls once arousing content plus pace exceeds capacity ([LC3MP record](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-17763054)) **[B, theory]**. Mayer's **redundancy principle (d = 0.86)** warns that narration plus identical on-screen text plus graphics underperforms narration plus graphics ([U-Mich PDF](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/um-multimedialearning-final.pdf)). Sound-off design pushes the other way, toward full captions. The resolution is short keyword supers rather than verbatim transcripts when sound is likely on, and fast cuts that do not each add new information. No platform data links cuts per second to view-through or recall.

## Motion helps when it carries the message and hurts when it decorates

Animation's learning advantage is real but modest. Höffler and Leutner's meta-analysis of 76 comparisons found **d = 0.37** overall. The advantage rises to **d = 0.40 for representational animation, d = 0.76 for realistic or video-based animation, and d = 1.06 for procedural knowledge** ([Leibniz-IPN](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis)) **[A]**. A larger and more recent meta-analysis (61 studies, N = 7,036) found a smaller **g = 0.226**. Effects were larger when the animation was system-paced (g = .31) and narrated (g = .34), and much larger when there was no accompanying on-screen text (g = .88) ([Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf)) **[A]**. Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt's sceptical review shows that many apparent animation wins compared unequal content. They proposed **Congruence** (use motion for things that change over time) and **Apprehension** (animations are often too fast or complex to perceive accurately) ([Tversky et al. 2002](https://faculty.washington.edu/aragon/classes/hcde411/w13/readings/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)) **[B]**. In advertising, animation reliably grabs attention but its effect on recall is mixed. One study found animated banners *harder* to remember than static ones ([Burke et al. 2004](https://thoughtcrumbs.com/publications/ToCHI04.pdf)), and animation had no effect during goal-directed tasks ([JAIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol22/iss1/2)) **[B, mixed]**. The widely repeated claim that "movement enhances recall by up to 40%" has no citable study behind it ([Sparkol blog](https://blog.sparkol.com/how-animated-words-make-emotional-connections)) **[unverified]**.

### Kinetic type conveys tone; animated charts dramatise one change

Kinetic typography reliably conveys emotion, even with neutral words ([KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201409864556113.do)). Perceived energy is driven by size, velocity, acceleration and the amount of movement ([GRID 2024](https://www.grid.uns.ac.rs/symposium/download/2024/89.pdf)) **[B/C]**. Eye-tracking shows that fluid kinetic type produces more saccades and shorter fixations ([UHasselt](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/handle/1942/32698)), which helps scanning but hurts reading if the timing is careless. No controlled study shows that kinetic type beats static type on recall in ads.

Data visualisation in motion has its own trap. The famous finding that a trivial chart makes claims more believable (Tal & Wansink 2014) **failed four replications**, which found text plus chart no more persuasive than text alone and sometimes less ([Dragicevic & Jansen 2018](https://hal.sorbonne-universite.fr/hal-01580259)). Its senior author has had 13 papers retracted for misconduct ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/retraction-of-a-journal-article-doesnt-make-its-findings-false-103829)) **[F]**. Better evidence shows that charts persuade people *without* strong prior opinions and are less effective against people who hold opposing views ([Pandey et al. 2014 via NYU CDS](https://cds.nyu.edu/?p=2017)) **[B]**. **Staged animated transitions improve perception of change over static charts** ([Heer & Robertson 2007](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/2007-AnimatedTrans-InfoVis.pdf)) **[B]**. Gapminder-style trend playback is engaging for presentation but error-prone for analysis ([Robertson et al. 2008](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf)) **[B]**. No peer-reviewed study tests count-up or number-ticker animations on credibility, so this is a genuine gap.

### SaaS launch videos share a style, but nobody has published their conversion results

Admired launch reels from Linear, Stripe, Raycast and others share a recognisable grammar. They run about 30–90 seconds, make the product UI the hero (often recreated or cropped for drama), carry one idea per video, use a restrained palette, eased and staggered motion, and little narration, and ship as vertical cutdowns with captions ([designmd.co](https://www.designmd.co/blog/linear-vs-vercel-motion); [Moonb](https://www.moonb.io/blog/product-launch-video)) **[C, blogs]**. **No source in this research attributes CTR, signups or conversion to any of these videos.** Their "high-performing" status rests on reputation. The style is nonetheless consistent with the research: UI-as-hero is representational and procedural motion (the largest effect sizes above), and one idea per video satisfies Apprehension. Platform motion guidelines converge on similar timing. Material Design puts mobile transitions around **300 ms** and warns that transitions over 400 ms can feel slow ([Material](https://m1.material.io/motion/duration-easing.html)). Apple's guidance says to add motion purposefully and never make it the sole carrier of important information ([Apple HIG](https://developer-mdn.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/foundations/motion)). The cartoon principles of slow-in/slow-out, anticipation and smooth arrivals entered UI design through Chang and Ungar ([UIST 1993](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247/s08/readings/Chang_AnimationInUI_UIST93.pdf)) **[guidelines; not effectiveness evidence]**. No peer-reviewed evidence shows that beat-synced editing improves recall or conversion, although Kantar lists tight music-synced editing among its short-format drivers ([Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/north-america/inspiration/advertising-media/best-practices-for-creative-effectiveness)) **[C]**.

Data on length and structure is vendor-sourced, and it measures engagement, not purchase. Wistia's dataset of 564,710 videos shows engagement holding to about two minutes and then dropping ([Wistia](https://wistia.com/blog/optimal-video-length)). A small social sample showed completion falling from **100% at 12 seconds to 74% at 18 seconds and 47% at 30 seconds** ([Wistia social](https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/overcoming-engagement-drop-on-social)) **[C]**. In Vidyard data, case-study videos average **36% engagement versus 23%** for product videos ([Whitehat SEO](https://whitehat-seo.co.uk/blog/unlocking-the-power-of-video)) **[C]**, which supports including a proof beat. The problem → solution → proof → CTA arc is a strong convention, but no study validates it.

## Each platform rewards native, vertical, early-branded creative

The platforms differ in sound defaults and attention style, but their advice converges on short, 9:16, captioned, hook-first and native-looking creative. Every figure below is first-party or aggregator data and should be read as **[C]**: large samples, self-interested sellers, rarely published methods.

Meta's most rigorous public study is the 2017 Facebook × MetrixLab analysis of **759 ads from 300+ brands**. Mobile-first ads (brand in the opening seconds, about 20 seconds long, built for sound off) beat TV ads adapted for mobile on recall, and only 38% of traditional ads showed the brand early ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebooks-published-a-new-study-which-highlights-effective-mobile-video-te/512753)) **[C, platform]**. Retention curves look the same everywhere: a cliff in the first 1–3 seconds, then decay. Vidyard's 1.78M business videos show **66% completion under one minute, falling to 22% beyond 20 minutes** ([MarketingProfs](https://marketingprofs.com/charts/2023/49949/business-video-benchmarks-retention-rates-by-length)). Off-feed, the curve inverts for conversion. On Wistia, videos over 30 minutes convert at 17% versus 2% for videos under 3 minutes, because only high-intent viewers stay ([Chief Marketer](https://chiefmarketer.com/wistia-state-of-video-report-engagement-down-content-under-promoted)) **[C, heavy selection bias]**. Short video and long video do different jobs: short buys reach and recognition, long converts people who are already interested.

What gets *shared* is better established than what gets clicked. In about 7,000 New York Times articles, **high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) increased virality and low-arousal sadness reduced it**. A one-standard-deviation increase in awe raised the odds of making the most-emailed list by about 30% ([Berger & Milkman](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-secret-to-online-success-what-makes-content-go-viral)) **[B; text news from 2008–09, not video]**. Rigorous comparisons of hook archetypes (question, bold claim, pattern interrupt, result-first) do not exist publicly, and every hook-type claim in circulation is anecdotal. On CTAs, Wistia reports a **16% response rate for clickable CTAs and 24% completion for embedded lead forms** on hosted video ([Chief Marketer](https://chiefmarketer.com/wistia-state-of-video-report-engagement-down-content-under-promoted)). The claim that mid-roll CTAs beat end CTAs comes from aggregators with unclear dates ([Opus Pro](https://www.opus.pro/de-de/blog/monetizing-videos-with-strategic-caption-placement-and-cta-integration)) **[C]**.

## The neuromarketing frameworks agree on four things and mostly validate their own tools

Google ABCD, System1, Nielsen, Ipsos, Meta, TikTok and Kantar converge on four principles: **hook immediately, integrate the brand into the attention-grabbing moment, make people feel something (humour, surprise, positive emotion), and give one clear next step.** The agreement is meaningful because the firms use different methods. It is undermined because nearly every one sells media or ad testing, and their outcome measures are usually their own predictive scores. Google's headline ABCD figures (+30% short-term sales likelihood, +17% long-term brand contribution) came from **Kantar's Link AI scoring about 11,000 ads against 180 features and correlating ABCD compliance with *predicted* outcomes**, not observed sales ([Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/north-america/industries/technology-and-telecoms/validating-googles-abcd-framework-with-the-power-of-artificial-intelligence)). The attribution in the footnote is internally inconsistent ([Think with Google](https://business.google.com/in/think/future-of-marketing/youtube-video-ad-creative/)). This is model-on-model evidence, commissioned by the media seller **[C]**.

System1's Star and Spike ratings link emotional intensity and fast brand recognition to long-term and short-term effects. In its Super Bowl analysis, ads with **brand characters averaged a 1.38 Spike versus 1.24 for celebrity ads** ([System1](https://system1group.com/blog/5-game-changing-creative-tips-for-your-100-yard-super-bowl-countdown)) **[C, no independent sales validation found]**. Orlando Wood's *Lemon* and *Look Out* argue that "left-brain" features have crowded out "right-brain" ones (sense of place, characters with agency, dialogue, melody), and that ads with fewer right-brain features rarely earn high Star ratings ([LBB Online](https://lbbonline.com/news/are-ads-becoming-too-far-left-brain); [Direct New Ideas](https://www.directnewideas.com/magazine/watch_out_advertising_is_becoming_increasingly_left_brained_/?id=3409)) **[C]**. This creates the sharpest tension in the evidence. Wood's left-brain list includes **on-screen words, voiceover, monologue, abstracted product close-ups and high rhythmic soundtracks**, which are exactly what Meta and TikTok recommend for short-form performance. The most likely explanation is different outcome metrics: platforms optimise recall and clicks, while System1 optimises emotion-predicted brand growth. The hemisphere neuroscience behind Wood's framing is contested and best read as a metaphor.

Nielsen's 2017 analysis of about 500 CPG campaigns attributed **47% of advertising-driven sales lift to creative**, against 22% for reach and 9% for targeting ([MarketingCharts](https://www.marketingcharts.com/television-80662)) **[C]**. The figure is frequently misquoted: it covers CPG only and advertising-driven lift only, not total sales. Nielsen's neuroscience lab found that **about 90% of neuro-optimised 15-second cuts tested as well as their 30-second originals** ([Nielsen 2014](https://www.nielsen.com/id/insights/2014/the-argument-for-more-effective-short-form-ads)) **[C]**. Kantar's database of 13,000+ ads names humour as the strongest receptivity enhancer and credits **showing product benefits with +10 points in persuasion** ([Kantar](https://www.kantar.com/north-america/inspiration/advertising-media/best-practices-for-creative-effectiveness)) **[C, self-validated]**. TikTok reports that native creator ads get **27% higher ad recall and up to 193% higher 6-second view-through** ([TikTok Works PDF](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/TikTok_Works_Recut_Remix_Reimagine.pdf)). Many of its other famous percentages (71% decide in 3 seconds, 40.1% more impressions for vertical) circulate only through blogs ([Sovran](https://sovran.ai/blog/tiktok-creative-best-practices)), and "impression lift" partly reflects TikTok's own delivery algorithm **[C]**. Ipsos frames effectiveness as "branded attention", but its public detail is thin product marketing ([Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-gh/creative-assessment)) **[C, under-sourced]**.

The peer-reviewed neuromarketing literature is more sobering. In the ARF-funded Venkatraman et al. study, **fMRI ventral-striatum activity was the best predictor of market-level ad elasticity beyond self-report** ([JMR 2015 PDF](https://stanford.edu/~knutson/nfc/venkatraman15.pdf)) **[B]**. That lab setup is far beyond a startup's budget. The ARF's vendor trial, meanwhile, found that neuromarketers' measures showed **"no common truth"** across vendors ([Varan et al. 2015](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/how-reliable-are-neuromarketers-measures-of-advertising-effective/)) **[B]**. EEG arousal can correlate with noticing an ad but negatively with liking it ([Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.00736/epub)), and reverse inference remains unresolved ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674591/)). Eye-tracking and moment-by-moment attention data are reliable enough to act on. Single-number "brain engagement" scores are not.

## Founders buy $29 spots for audience fit, verifiable clicks and a story

The Surviver buyer is unusually sceptical. Founders say the launch circuit sends the wrong audience: IndieMerger's #3 Product Hunt finish produced 1,305 visitors but converted at **0.5%, against 1.3% for its organic traffic** ([Product Hunt forum](https://www.producthunt.com/p/indiemerger/we-placed-3rd-on-product-hunt-and-earned-543)). Another founder called upvotes "internet points" ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/why-launching-on-product-hunt-might-not-be-worth-it-for-you-e78f256ae3)). One founder listed on about 30 directories saw roughly four visits a month, and commenters called most directories "DA farms for the directory" ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/which-launch-directories-actually-sent-you-users-not-just-a-backlink-42e82c104f)) **[C, anecdote]**. Trust in vote counts is damaged. A WakaTime analysis of public Product Hunt data claimed that **more than 60% of signups are bots** and that bot upvotes have exceeded real ones since 2022, though the author acknowledges methodological limits ([WakaTime](https://wakatime.com/blog/67-bots-so-many-bots)) **[C, semi-systematic]**. Buyers of newsletter slots ask for stats screenshots and per-slot click counts, and one founder concluded that opens and clicks matter more than subscriber totals ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/ive-spent-2-000-sponsoring-newsletters-and-here-s-what-i-ve-learned-so-far-085a751fc4)) **[C]**. That founder's reference price is useful: **about $100 per newsletter ad after negotiation.**

At $29 the purchase is a personal-card decision far below any approval threshold. Corporate card programs routinely allow in-policy purchases of hundreds of dollars without finance sign-off ([Wallester](https://wallester.com/blog/business-insights/spend-limits-and-controls-how-businesses-set-smarter-rules-for-corporate-spending)) **[C]**. The real barrier is **wasted effort and looking foolish**, which matches the CEB/Google personal-value finding. Risk reversal cuts both ways. Guarantees lower perceived risk on average, but a prominently added 90-day guarantee reportedly preceded a double-digit sales drop for KitchenAid because it introduced doubt ([Michel Fortin](https://michelfortin.com/copywriting/risk-reversal/)) **[C, anecdote]**. The closest analogue to Surviver, outbid.lol, claims **705+ paying companies and $120K in 48 hours** from a leaderboard showing each listing's paid amount and tracked clicks, with 10+ imitators within a day ([Macau Business press release](https://macaubusiness.com/120000-and-one-million-visitors-in-48-hours-for-solo-founders-side-project-outbid-lol/)) **[C, self-reported PR]**. It shows that spectacle, status and visible numbers can sell spots to founders. It also means Surviver will be compared with "another outbid clone" unless it differentiates.

Competition and ranking are a credible motivator. In experiments, leaderboards work like difficult goals, and people implicitly aim near the top ([Landers et al. via NeoAcademic](https://neoacademic.com/2015/09/30/gamification-works-a-lot-like-goal-setting/)) **[B]**. A 2025 replication found that leaderboards improved quantity but not quality of performance ([Masaryk University](https://www.muni.cz/en/research/publications/2495997)). A meta-analysis found small motivational (g = .36) and behavioural (g = .25) effects that were less stable under rigorous designs ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)) **[A, education contexts]**. Novelty effects are the standard caveat ([Hamari et al. 2014](https://research.aalto.fi/fi/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o/)). Two major gaps remain in the public literature. **No data shows whether founders value YouTube Live or Discord exposure specifically**, and no study examines leaderboards as a reason to *buy* a placement. Surviver's own audience data, covered in the next section, fills part of the first gap. What it does not yet show is how many stream viewers click through to a featured product. The formats that earn engagement in founder communities are first-person postmortems with real numbers; every high-engagement source in this research was one ([Indie Hackers analysis of 481+ interviews](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/11-marketing-channels-that-consistently-work-for-founders-rBltLPaWjLQ9xHZiSqOW)).

## Surviver's own audience: about 400K monthly views from the people who use these tools

**Owner-reported facts (added after the research round).** The Surviver owner streams daily on YouTube (@rounieee) to about **400,000 monthly viewers**, described as developers, entrepreneurs and students who use the latest AI tools. Every product in the season is shown to that audience on stream every day. The channel's public figures are consistent with that total: **about 13.4K average views per stream across 29 streams a month comes to roughly 389K views a month**, with 8.03K subscribers (read from youtube.com/@rounieee/streams on 2026-10-06). One caveat is that 400K is a count of *views*. The number of *unique* viewers is lower, and only YouTube Studio's unique-viewer report would show it. **[owner-reported; channel stats public]**

This changes the analysis in four ways.

1. **It answers founders' main objection: wrong audience.** The strongest pattern in the founder evidence was that launch channels send the wrong people. Examples are Product Hunt traffic converting at 0.5% against 1.3% organic, directories called "DA farms", and founders selling to other founders. A stream watched by AI-tool users is an audience of *category buyers*. Ehrenberg-Bass research holds that this audience matters most, because mental availability is built among the people who buy in the category. Surviver's pitch is therefore no longer "a launch site". It is **"your product, shown to the people who use products like yours."** **[B for the principle / I for this audience]**
2. **Daily exposure turns one launch spike into repeated exposure.** Founders complain that launch traffic is a one-day spike that vanishes. Thirty daily appearances across a season is the repeated, spaced exposure that the mere-exposure (r = .26), repetition-truth and mental-availability findings reward. The inverted-U warning applies to the *stream* too: rotate how products are presented (rails, a spotlight segment, chat moments) so that day 25 does not feel like day 1. **[A for exposure / I for stream application]**
3. **The reach comparison becomes the anchor.** The founder evidence's reference prices were about $100 per newsletter ad and roughly 1,305 visitors from a #3 Product Hunt finish. Against that, "$29 for a season in front of ~400K monthly views" is a strong anchor. The comparison must stay honest, however. Stream *views* are not *visits* to the product, and a tile in a side rail gets far less attention than a full-screen feature: the attention research suggests seconds, not minutes. The ad should therefore claim exposure ("seen on stream by ~400K monthly views") and let verified clicks on the leaderboard prove traffic. It must not imply 400K visitors. **[I, built on C evidence]**
4. **Credibility now hinges on proving the audience.** This audience distrusts unverifiable numbers. The 400K and "AI-tool users" claims should be backed on screen and on the site, for example with a YouTube Studio screenshot of monthly views and viewer geography and age. Real chat reactions to a featured product also help. Stream footage showing a product on air with chat responding is the single most persuasive proof beat available, because it shows audience and fit at the same time. **[C founder evidence / I]**

**New gap to close with Surviver's own data:** clicks from the stream per product per day. Tag each product's link from the stream description or chat with a source parameter, so the season postmortem can report "stream → clicks" alongside "board → clicks". That is the number founders will ask for after the first season.

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes the question. The decisive variable is not whether an ad uses motion graphics but whether its first two seconds create a recognisable, believable reason to keep watching, and whether the brand is attached to that moment. Motion graphics are a strong medium for Surviver for one research-backed reason: the product *is* a change over time (ranks moving, clicks accumulating, a season unfolding). That is precisely the congruent, representational motion where animation's effect sizes are largest, and very little SaaS advertising has a product that naturally moves. The biggest risk is credibility, not attention. The audience has watched Product Hunt fill with bots and dozens of outbid clones appear overnight, so any number that looks inflated, any count-up that is not real, or any urgency timer that feels manufactured will trigger the same scepticism that makes them distrust the competition.

The industry frameworks are better read as a consensus checklist than as proof. Their effect sizes are mostly vendors grading their own homework, and the strongest-sounding claims (85% muted, charts boost belief, 40% recall gains, unconscious priming) are the weakest underneath. The best practical substitute for missing evidence is Surviver's own data. With 32 products, real click tracking and a recurring stream, Surviver can run the hook-type, length and CTA A/B tests the public literature lacks, then publish the results as a founder-native postmortem. That would close the research gaps and sell the next season. Surviver's distribution is its decisive differentiator: about 400K monthly views from the AI-tool users who are the products' own market, every day of the season. That is what separates it from outbid-style boards, which sell rank and not an audience. The ads should make that audience visible and verifiable, then let the real click data do the convincing.

## Playbook: designing high-converting motion-graphics ads for Surviver.lol

Grades: **A** = meta-analytic or multi-lab replicated. **B** = replicated primary, peer-reviewed field or eye-tracking work, or large databank. **C** = platform, vendor or anecdotal evidence. **I** = inference from evidence, untested in ads; validate by A/B test.

| # | Principle | How to apply it for Surviver.lol | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open on a change, not a logo** | Frame 1: a leaderboard row jumps up a rank, or the stream frame pushes in toward the viewer. Motion onset and looming capture attention; continuous ambient motion is weaker. | B (lab) / I (ads) |
| 2 | **Land brand + proposition in 0–2 s** | By ~1.5 s, show the Surviver wordmark small and integrated into the leaderboard UI with a 3–5 word super ("32 SaaS. Ranked by real clicks."). Most value accrues before a view counts. | C |
| 3 | **Pulse the brand; no big opening slate** | Show the brand briefly 3–4 times (UI chrome, stream overlay, end frame) rather than one long block. Pulsing cut zapping 8–10%; big early logos raise skips. | B |
| 4 | **Make the product's own motion the hero** | Animate the actual leaderboard: ranks reorder, click counters tick, a product enters the stream. Representational and realistic motion carries the largest animation gains (d ≈ 0.40–0.76). Avoid decorative particles and transitions. | A (learning) / I |
| 5 | **Every number must be real and auditable** | Use actual season click counts, the real subscriber count (8.03K), average views (~13.4K per stream) and monthly views (~400K) with a source cue ("live YouTube stats"). Say "views", not "viewers", unless YouTube Studio's unique-viewer figure backs it. Never fake count-ups: this audience distrusts inflated traffic, and count-up credibility is untested. | C (founder evidence) / I |
| 6 | **Use quantity scarcity and demand cues, not countdown timers** | "32 spots. 9 left." plus "X founders joined this week". Demand and quantity scarcity outperform pure time pressure, which showed no significant effect. Use only true numbers. | A (moderated) |
| 7 | **Anchor $29 against what founders already pay** | "A full season for less than one ~$100 newsletter ad." Anchoring is robust for numeric comparisons. $29 already gets the left-digit benefit, so $28 or $29.99 adds nothing. | A / B |
| 8 | **Frame loss modestly** | "Your launch deserves more than a one-day spike" works, but expect loss framing to weigh about 2× gains, not miracles. Avoid fear-heavy copy. | A (λ ≈ 1.96) |
| 9 | **Lead with personal value and story** | Sell the screenshot moment: "Your product, live on stream, climbing a public leaderboard." Status, fun and a story to post on X outweigh business value in B2B buying. | B (large purchases) / I |
| 10 | **Design one peak and a strong end** | Peak: a product rockets up the board, or appears on the live stream with chat reacting. End: a clean frame with brand, "$29 · 32 spots", and one CTA. Peak and end dominate memory (r = .58); length matters little. | A |
| 11 | **One idea per ad; keyword supers only** | One claim per ad (real clicks, OR live stream, OR 32 spots). Supers of 3–5 words held 1.5–2 s, at most ~42 characters per line, slower than 15–20 cps. Coherence (d = .86) and redundancy (d = .86) penalise clutter. | A (learning) / guideline |
| 12 | **Signal with highlight, not explanation** | Use a cursor, outline or colour pulse to guide the eye to the row that moved. The site's flat pastel accents can serve as the highlight colour. Signalling gains are small but reliable (d = .41; r = .17). | A |
| 13 | **Stage complex motion, one primary move at a time** | Rescale, then reorder, then label. Stagger secondary elements. Use eased (slow-in/slow-out) curves and keep UI-scale moves ~200–400 ms; hold hero moments longer. | B / guideline |
| 14 | **Build for sound-off, reward sound-on** | Captions and visuals carry the full message. Add a short, recognisable sonic sting and stream ambience (chat pings) for TikTok and YouTube, which are sound-on. Ignore the 2016 "85% muted" stat and the unverified 8.53× sonic claim. | C |
| 15 | **Use a founder face with gaze toward the board** | In UGC-style cuts, have the founder look or point at the leaderboard rather than stare into the lens. Gaze cues direct attention and raised purchase intent in banners. | B (banners) / I (video) |
| 16 | **Make it native per platform** | TikTok/Reels/Shorts: 9:16, 15–30 s, lo-fi founder plus screen capture variants. LinkedIn: 15–30 s, captioned, logo in 2 s. X: under 15 s, native 9:16 upload. YouTube and site: 45–90 s leaderboard-and-stream explainer. | C |
| 17 | **Include a proof beat** | 3–5 s of real stream footage or a past product's click-and-rank result ("#4 last season: 1,2xx real clicks"). Proof content out-engages generic product video, and founders want evidence of results. | C |
| 18 | **One clear CTA, repeated on screen near the end** | "Claim a spot — surviver.lol". State the offer early for scrollers, repeat it at the end, and use the platform's CTA button. Direction is one of the ABCD four. | C |
| 19 | **Use high-arousal positive emotion; avoid the sad-founder framing** | Awe at a rank jump, playful competition, mild frustration with "internet points" upvotes. High-arousal emotion drives sharing; low-arousal sadness suppresses it. | B (text news) |
| 20 | **Build a fluent, consistent device** | Reuse one recurring motif (the rank-jump animation, a mascot or a sonic sting) across every cut and every season so it gains fame and uniqueness. Brand characters and fluent devices score highest in vendor data. | B / C |
| 21 | **Differentiate from outbid clones explicitly** | Say "fixed $29, no bidding war, a whole season, live stream + Discord" in one super. Founders will otherwise file Surviver under "another pay-for-a-spot site". | C / I |
| 22 | **Rotate creative and test hooks on a fixed body** | Keep 3–5 hook variants (result-first, problem-first, live-stream clip, number-first) on one identical body. No public evidence ranks hook types, so your own data decides. Rotate before wear-out. | A (wear-out exists) / I |
| 23 | **Publish your own postmortem as the long-form ad** | After each season, cut a numbers-heavy video and post ("what #1 got vs #32"). This is the format founder communities reward, and it doubles as social proof. | C |
| 24 | **Lead with the audience, not the slot** | The hook names who will see the product: "Show your product to ~400K monthly views of devs and founders using AI tools." Pair it with "every day, all season" to answer the one-day-spike objection. Claim exposure on stream and prove traffic with verified clicks; never imply 400K visits. | B (category buyers) / I |
| 25 | **Don't lean on debunked levers** | No subliminal colour priming, no decoy pricing tiers shown visually, no "trust me" chart decoration, no generic "75% of founders…" social-proof claims. Each failed or shrank under replication. | F |

A default 20-second vertical spot built from these principles runs as follows. **0–1.5 s:** a leaderboard row snaps up three ranks (or, as the audience-first hook variant from principle 24, the live stream with chat reacting to a product), a click counter ticks, and a small Surviver mark sits in the board header. **1.5–5 s:** the super "32 SaaS. Ranked by real clicks." **5–11 s:** a push-in to the live-stream frame showing a product on air with real chat, captioned "Live every day · ~400K monthly views of devs & founders" (views, not unique or concurrent viewers, so word it exactly). **11–15 s:** the proof beat, a past product's real rank and clicks. **15–20 s:** the end frame "$29 · a full season · 9 of 32 spots left" with the CTA and the sonic sting. Every number on screen comes from live data. Test it against a founder-to-camera variant that uses the same body.
